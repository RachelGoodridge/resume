The Big Bird Hybrid Lineage in Darwin’s Finches

Hybridization can introduce novel genetic variation that alters evolutionary trajectories and may play an important role in rapid speciation during adaptive radiations. In the Darwin’s finch radiation, hybridization between species is traceable through heritable variation in beak morphology, reflected in increasing morphological and genomic similarity between the medium ground finch, Geospiza fortis, and the common cactus finch, G. scandens, on the small island Daphne Major. However, the evolutionary outcomes of gene flow can be unpredictable, with rare events sometimes leaving lasting genomic signatures. In 1981, a single male G. conirostris, a cactus finch with a characteristically larger, deeper beak, made a rare long-distance migration to Daphne and founded a reproductively isolated hybrid lineage with G. fortis, known as the “Big Bird” lineage. The long-term fate of this lineage has remained unknown. We used whole-genome data from 2022 to assess the genetic legacy of this lineage in the contemporary population, building on over 30 years of previous genomic monitoring. Using identity-by-descent (IBD) tracts with the founder, we show that while the Big Bird lineage remained reproductively isolated from its parental species, it has more recently outbred with the scandens population. Remarkably, over 70% of scandens individuals now share at least one IBD segment with the founder, enabling reconstruction of approximately 80% of the founder’s genome. Consistent with a phenotypic effect of introgression, individuals with a higher fraction of their genome in IBD tend to exhibit blunter beaks. Together, these results reveal the extraordinary genetic legacy of a single immigrant.

A manuscript describing this work is in preparation.

Genomic Imputation Pipeline for Low-Coverage WGS Data

Low-coverage whole-genome sequencing (lcWGS) reduces per-sample sequencing costs while still providing genome-wide data. However, the low depth (typically 1–4x) means that many genotypes are uncertain or missing, requiring imputation to produce high-quality variant calls suitable for downstream analysis.
I developed a Snakemake-based pipeline for genotype imputation of lcWGS data, built around the GLIMPSE1 toolkit. The pipeline handles the full workflow from raw input fastq files through mapping to the reference genome, chunking a high-coverage reference panel, imputing genotypes, and merging results into a final phased and imputed VCF. It is modular and configurable, and produces output ready for downstream tools such as PLINK, ADMIXTURE, and IBIS.
This pipeline was developed to support the Big Bird project but is written to be generalizable to other non-model organisms with an appropriate reference panel.

Multilocus Suppression Gene Drive

Gene drives are selfish genetic elements that can spread through a population faster than standard Mendelian inheritance, making them a powerful potential tool for population suppression or modification. However, single-locus drives face challenges from resistance alleles that can prevent drive spread. We propose a new suppression drive designed to reduce the selective advantage of functional resistance alleles by targeting multiple distant-site genes, each with only a moderate recessive deleterious effect. As these recessive deleterious mutations accumulate, individuals increasingly inherit mutations in a homozygous state, causing a substantial genetic load to be imposed on the population.
This project, developed in collaboration with the Messer Lab, investigates these dynamics using forward-time population genetics simulations in SLiM. The work explores how the number of target loci, the fitness costs of target sites, and linkage at target sites affect the realized genetic load of the population and recovery time.

Developing a Recommendation System for Wine Enthusiasts

The purpose of this project is to create a wine recommendation system that allows users of all experience levels to explore new options and make decisions about their next wine purchase. The dataset includes 49,546 unique wine tastings and descriptions written by sommeliers. Using text preprocessing, dimensionality reduction, and topic modeling, I created a content-based filtering system that finds the closest match to any given wine in the dataset. The search can be narrowed based on user input so the algorithm returns only wines with specific requested features.

Predicting Potentially Hazardous Asteroids

Near-Earth Objects (NEOs) occasionally have the potential to collide with Earth and cause enormous damage. Using NASA’s Small-Body Database, I trained a machine learning classification model to predict which asteroids are potentially hazardous. Following oversampling to address class imbalance and optimization of parameters, the best model based on F1 validation scores was XGBoost Classifier (holdout score: 0.784). The most important features were absolute magnitude, closest distance to the sun, and orbit uncertainty.

Background Information

Caenorhabditis elegans is a millimeter-long roundworm (nematode) found worldwide in soil and rotting plant matter. It serves as a model organism for various genetic and evolutionary studies due to its fast life cycle, ease of maintenance, and simple neurological structure. Click here to watch a brief C. elegans video.

C. elegans Life History Model

I conducted research in the lab of Dr. Scott Rifkin studying the evolution of the dauer stage, an intermediate larval stage during which the worm can enter a stasis-like state given difficult environmental conditions such as overcrowding or lack of food. I developed a population ecology and evolution model in Python to simulate the development of a group of individuals, their interactions with each other, and their responses to specific environmental conditions. The model accounts for genetics and stochasticity in decision-making and was validated against laboratory experiments.

The complete Python script and a Jupyter notebook demonstrating use of the model can be found here.

Abstract of the Thesis

Nematodes such as Caenorhabditis elegans, C. briggsae, C. remanei, and C. nigoni are model organisms that primarily reside in rotting fruit and plant matter. When conditions are poor, worms must decide whether to enter the dauer stage. Entering dauer allows worms to survive for months, but the risk of dying in dauer is high and this creates a tradeoff. I created an extensive model in Python to simulate population dynamics and decision-making strategies of worms under various environmental conditions. This model includes genes related to both dauer and travel direction decision-making. Experiments showed significant evolution of both genes under stronger selection against worms in dauer, both genes under higher food availability, the dauer gene only when the dauer genotype-to-phenotype mapping is altered, and neither gene when seasonality is introduced. Results suggest that the direction of evolution depends on the nature and strength of environmental selection pressures.

Larval Dietary Restriction Experiment

I designed an experiment with four treatment groups of L1 (first larval stage) worms, each fed a different concentration of E. coli (6 mg/mL, 4.8 mg/mL, 3.6 mg/mL, or 2.4 mg/mL), to test whether the quantity of food available to a worm determines the timing of its transition to the next developmental stage. I used a strain of C. elegans with a GFP marker on the molting gene to identify molting events under an epifluorescent microscope. I first validated the experimental design using simulation, then conducted the experiment in the laboratory. Results showed that larval dietary restriction does lengthen the amount of time spent in the larval stage, meaning that developmental timing is food-dependent in larvae, consistent with what is known for adults.
